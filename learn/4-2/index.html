Lesson 4-2: Information Design and User Experience for Websites

How do designers plan a website around its users, and what principles make a page clear and easy to use?

A website that makes sense to its designer can still confuse its visitors. Good design therefore begins with the user, not the creator. Designers first picture a real user (a persona), sketch where information will go before choosing any colours (a wireframe), and follow four simple principles, contrast, repetition, alignment and proximity, so the page is clear at a glance. This user-first mindset is called user-centered design.

Key Fact. Good web design starts from the user: a persona clarifies who they are, a wireframe fixes what goes where, the CRAP principles make it clear, and user-centered design keeps every choice on the user's side.

1. Persona

A persona is a typical user image of those who will use the website, set up as a concrete person. Details such as age, occupation, purpose and challenges are selected.

By setting up a persona, designers can think specifically about "what this user needs", and the direction of the design becomes less likely to drift.

The textbook's own example: a person in their 30s, an office worker, who wants to compare products on a smartphone during the commute, and wants to obtain information in a short time.

GOING DEEPER

Why a person and not a group.

"Our users are everyone aged 18 to 60" gives a design team nothing to decide with. Every argument about the site can then be settled by whoever speaks loudest. A persona replaces that with a single person who has a purpose and a problem, so a question like "should the price be bigger?" becomes "would a commuter comparing products on a phone find this price quickly?", which has an answer.

Notice what the book's example contains and what it does not. It contains age, occupation, purpose and a challenge. It does not contain the person's name, their favourite colour, or anything about the server. The assessment book tests exactly this: the performance of the website's server is not appropriate information for a persona, because a persona describes the user, not the system.

الـ persona يعني تحط المستخدم كشخص واحد محدد، مش "كل الناس". سنه، شغله، إيه اللي جايبه للموقع، وإيه اللي بيصعّب عليه. لما يبقى قدامك شخص واحد، أي خلاف على التصميم بيتحل بسؤال: الشخص ده هيلاقي اللي عايزه ولا لأ؟ ولاحظ إن أداء السيرفر مش جزء من الـ persona، لأنها بتوصف المستخدم مش النظام.

2. Wireframe

A wireframe is a rough blueprint of the layout of a web page. It does not include colour or design decoration; it is created to determine the placement and priority of information.

By using a wireframe, "what information to place where" can be considered separately from visual design. If decorations such as colours and fonts are decided first, discussions tend to drift toward personal preferences in appearance, so finalizing the placement and priority of information first makes it easier for stakeholders to reach agreement.

GOING DEEPER

The two reasons, and the one students forget.

The first is about the work. Structure and appearance are different problems, and solving them at the same time means solving neither well. The wireframe settles structure on its own terms.

The second is about the people. Nobody has a strong personal opinion about whether the opening hours belong above or below the map, but everybody has an opinion about the shade of blue. A discussion that starts with colour never finishes. A discussion that starts with a wireframe ends in an agreement that the visual designer can then work inside.

Students almost always write the first reason and leave out the second. The second is the one the textbook puts in its own Exam-Style Question, under "Refer to: reaching agreement with stakeholders".

الـ wireframe رسمة بالقلم الرصاص: إيه مكانه فين، ومين الأهم. من غير ألوان ولا خطوط ولا زخرفة. وفيه سببين لده، والطلبة بيكتبوا الأول وبينسوا التاني: الأول إن الترتيب والشكل مشكلتين مختلفتين، والتاني إن الناس بتتفق على الترتيب بسهولة لكن بتتخانق على اللون. اللي بيبدأ باللون ما بيخلصش.

PAUSE AND THINK · PAGE 83

Why decide where information goes (wireframe) before choosing colours and fonts?

Because placement and priority are decisions about the user's needs, while colour and font are decisions about appearance, and the first has to be settled before the second has anything to dress. Deciding the layout first also keeps the discussion off personal taste, so the people involved can agree on the structure instead of arguing about the decoration. Once everyone has agreed what goes where and what matters most, the visual design has a fixed brief to work to, and a change of colour later costs nothing, while a change of structure later means starting again.

3. The Four Principles of Design (CRAP Principles)

Designs that are easy to view and understand follow these four principles.

PrincipleDescription (purpose) Specific techniques and examples
Contrast Make the priority of information clear and immediately highlight the most important elements Make headings large and bold to differentiate them from body text. Use accent colours for important buttons, for example purchase.
Repetition Repeat specific characteristics so that the whole design has consistency, allowing users to navigate without getting lost Standardize the colour, size and font of headings on each page. Match the design of bullet point icons.
Alignment Align the position of each element to a common baseline, giving order to the entire screen Align the left edges of text and images precisely. Make a consistent rule for whether to use centre or left alignment.
Proximity Place related information close together so that it can be perceived as a single group at a glance Group product images, names and prices together. Leave appropriate spacing (margins) between items.
GOING DEEPER

One verb for each principle.

The four are easy to mix up because all four make a page look better. They are easy to separate if you ask what each one actually does to the content, in one verb:

  • Contrast highlights. Something becomes more important than the things around it.
  • Repetition unifies. Things in different places become recognisably the same family.
  • Alignment orders. Things line up on a shared edge, so the screen stops looking scattered.
  • Proximity groups. Things that belong together are read as one item.

Then test any improvement against the four verbs. "Standardize the heading font across all pages" unifies, so it is repetition. "Put the price under the product photo with no gap" groups, so it is proximity. The pair that students confuse is repetition and proximity, because both deal with more than one element at a time; the difference is that repetition is about sameness across the site and proximity is about closeness on the page.

أربع مبادئ، وأسهل طريقة تفرق بينهم إنك تسأل الحركة بتعمل إيه بالظبط: contrast بتـبرز، repetition بتـوحّد، alignment بتـرتّب، proximity بتـجمّع. والاتنين اللي بيتلخبطوا هما repetition و proximity: الأولى تشابه عبر الصفحات، والتانية قرب جوه الصفحة.

PAUSE AND THINK · PAGE 84

Which principle groups related items together, and which makes the most important item stand out?

Proximity groups related items together: product images, names and prices are placed close to each other with space left between the groups, so each group is perceived as a single item at a glance. Contrast makes the most important item stand out: a heading is made large and bold so it is clearly different from the body text, and an accent colour is used for an important button such as purchase, so the priority of the information is immediately visible.

4. User-Centered Design (UCD)

User-Centered Design (UCD) is a design approach that designs websites and services from the perspective of the user, rather than the convenience of the creator.

In UCD, design begins from "what the user needs" and "in what situation it will be used". Setting up a persona is one specific way of practising UCD; by clarifying the user image, design decisions can be kept consistently aligned with the user's perspective.

Improving usability for users and accessibility for a wide range of people are important goals of UCD.

GOING DEEPER

Usability and accessibility are not the same word.

Usability is how easily users can use the site and achieve their goals: how few steps it takes, how quickly the thing they came for is found, how rarely they make a mistake.

Accessibility is whether a wide range of people can use it at all: someone with low vision, someone who cannot use a mouse, someone on a slow connection or an old phone.

A site can have good usability for most visitors and still have bad accessibility, which is why the textbook names both as goals of UCD rather than treating one as part of the other. The assessment book asks the difference directly.

الـ UCD مش تقنية، دي زاوية نظر: القرار بيتاخد من ناحية المستخدم مش من ناحية اللي بيبني. وهدفها حاجتين مختلفتين: usability يعني يستخدمه بسهولة ويوصل للي عايزه، وaccessibility يعني يقدر يستخدمه أصلًا، زي حد نظره ضعيف أو عنده نت بطيء. ممكن موقع يبقى سهل لمعظم الناس وبرضه مقفول في وش ناس تانية.

EGYPTIAN EXAMPLE

Take a school results page that parents use. The persona is a working parent who opens the page once a term on a phone, in a hurry, and wants one number. The wireframe decides that the student's name and grade go at the top and the school news goes at the bottom, before anyone chooses a colour. Contrast makes the grade the largest thing on the screen. Proximity keeps the subject name and its mark together instead of in two separate columns. Repetition gives every term's page the same heading style, so a parent who used it last term does not have to learn it again. And accessibility means the grade is still readable for a parent with weak eyesight, which is why the size is not decided by what looks elegant.

Exam-Style Question

FROM THE TEXTBOOK

Explain why a designer creates a persona and a wireframe before choosing colours and fonts. [6]

Refer to: the user's needs, and reaching agreement with stakeholders.

Model answer, one mark per sentence.

  1. Good design begins from the user and not from the creator, so the first step is to find out who the user is.
  2. A persona is a typical user set up as a concrete person, with age, occupation, purpose and challenges, so the team can think specifically about what this user needs.
  3. With the persona fixed, the direction of the design is less likely to drift away from that user.
  4. The wireframe comes next and fixes the placement and the priority of the information, with no colour or decoration, so what goes where is decided separately from how it will look.
  5. This helps the stakeholders reach agreement, because if colours and fonts are decided first, the discussion tends to drift toward personal preferences in appearance.
  6. Colours and fonts are therefore chosen last, once the user's needs and the structure are already agreed, so every visual decision serves a user who is already known.

Where the marks are lost. An answer that only defines a persona and a wireframe earns two marks out of six. The question asks why they come first, so four of the marks are in the two reasons: the design stops drifting away from the user, and the stakeholders can agree on the structure before anyone argues about colour. The second reason is the one that is almost always missing, and the book puts it in its own Refer to list, so it is not optional.

Lesson Question, Answered

How do designers plan a website around its users, and what principles make a page clear and easy to use?

Design begins from the user, not the creator. A persona pictures a typical user as a concrete person, with age, occupation, purpose and challenges, so the team can focus on what that user needs. A wireframe then fixes the placement and priority of information before any colour or decoration, which keeps discussion on structure and helps stakeholders agree. To make the page clear, four principles are applied: contrast to highlight priority, repetition for consistency, alignment for order, and proximity to group related items. All of this is guided by user-centered design (UCD), which starts from what the user needs and aims to improve usability and accessibility. Good design, therefore, is planned from the user inward: who they are, what goes where, and how to make it clear.

Key Takeaway. Persona (who) then wireframe (where) then CRAP (make it clear) then UCD (decide for the user). Design from the user inward.
