Lesson 4-3: Methods for Evaluating Websites

How can a website be evaluated, and why is it useful to combine numbers with users' voices?

A website can be launched, look finished, and still fail the people who use it. The only way to know is to evaluate it, and there are two different ways to do that. You can count: how many people came, how long they stayed, how many bought something. Or you can watch and ask: sit beside a real user, see where they hesitate, and hear in their own words what confused them. The first gives you numbers. The second gives you reasons. Numbers tell you how much is happening; users' voices tell you why. Teams that evaluate well use both, and they use them in a particular order.

Key Fact. Evaluation comes in two kinds. Quantitative evaluation answers how much with numbers. Qualitative evaluation answers why with users' voices and behaviour. The accurate picture comes from combining them.

1. Qualitative and Quantitative Evaluation

Methods for evaluation generally fall into two main categories, qualitative evaluation and quantitative evaluation. These two categories are not special to websites. They are used across many fields, and the same ideas apply when a website is the thing being evaluated.

Evaluation methodDescription (purpose) Specific techniques
Qualitative evaluation Explore in depth the reasons behind users' psychology and behaviour, and evaluate qualitative aspects that cannot be expressed in numbers. Usability testing: observe actual operations to identify points that are hard to use.
Interviews: hear users' direct voices to understand their needs and dissatisfactions.
Quantitative evaluation Use numerical data such as access counts and time, and evaluate the current state objectively and statistically. Web analytics: analyse metrics such as PV count and time spent.
A/B testing: compare two options to verify which leads to better results.

Qualitative evaluation is suited to exploring the cause of "why is it hard to use", while quantitative evaluation is suited to grasping the overall trend of "how much is it being used". By combining both, more accurate evaluation is possible.

الفرق كله في سؤالين: قد إيه؟ دي quantitative، وليه؟ دي qualitative. ولو إجابة السؤال ممكن تتكتب رقم يبقى كمي، ولو إجابته جملة بيقولها حد يبقى كيفي.

PAUSE & THINK

Would counting visits tell you why people leave a page? Which method would?

No. A visit count tells you how many people arrived and how many left, and that is all it can ever tell you. It is a number, and a number has no reason attached to it. Two completely different problems, a confusing menu and a page that loads too slowly, produce exactly the same count.

The method that answers why is qualitative: a usability test, where you watch a real user try the task and see the moment they hesitate, or an interview, where you ask them directly what they expected to happen. The counting still matters, because it is what told you which page to go and watch.

How a site is actually evaluated

The textbook gives the two methods as a table and says in one line that combining them is better. It never draws the order in which a team uses them, and that order is what the six-mark question is really asking for.

GOING DEEPER

Why not just use the one that gives reasons, since reasons are what you actually want? Because of scale. Web analytics watches every visitor, all day, for free, and it never forgets. A usability test watches five people for an hour and costs a morning of work. So the numbers are the only method that can see the whole site at once, and the voices are the only method that can explain any part of it. Using voices alone means guessing which page to investigate; using numbers alone means knowing exactly where the problem is and never finding out what it is.

This also explains the order in the figure above. You do not interview people and then go looking for a number to support it. You let the numbers choose the question, then spend the expensive method on the exact spot that deserves it.

2. Major Indicators in Web Analytics

Web analytics is a technique for analysing how a website is accessed using numerical values. It is the central method of quantitative evaluation for websites. The indicators below are the ones commonly used, and the three the lesson names are the three you are expected to know.

IndicatorMeaningWhat it is used for
PV (Page View) The total number of times a page has been viewed. A basic indicator that measures how widely the site is seen and how much exposure it gets.
Bounce rate The proportion of visits that ended after viewing only the first page. An indicator of whether the page content matched the user's expectations and whether further actions were prompted.
Conversion rate (CVR) The proportion of visits that achieved a target action, such as a purchase or a registration. A very important indicator that measures how much the site contributes to business results (profits).

Notice what separates them. PV is a count: a whole number that only goes up. Bounce rate and CVR are proportions: a share of the visits, which can rise while the count falls, or fall while the count rises. That difference is what makes the next box possible.

التلاتة بالبلدي: PV الصفحة اتفتحت كام مرة (عدد). Bounce rate نسبة اللي دخلوا وخرجوا من أول صفحة. CVR نسبة اللي عملوا الحاجة اللي الموقع متعمل عشانها: اشترى، سجّل، حجز.

GOING DEEPER

The pattern the exam loves: high PV with a low CVR. Many people arrive; almost nobody does the thing the site exists for. The number tells you the arrival is not the problem, so the problem is somewhere between arriving and acting, and the numbers cannot say where. That is the signal to go and watch five people try it.

Read the opposite pattern too. A low PV with a high CVR means the few people who find the site are exactly the right people: the problem is not the site, it is that nobody knows it exists. Same site, same work, completely different fix. This is why one indicator on its own is never a verdict.

PAUSE & THINK

Match the question to the method: "how many?" against "why?"

How many? goes to quantitative evaluation: web analytics for PV, bounce rate and CVR, and A/B testing when you want to know which of two versions performs better.

Why? goes to qualitative evaluation: usability testing to see where users struggle, interviews to hear what they wanted, and heuristic evaluation for an expert's reading of the interface.

A quick test you can apply to any exam sentence: if the answer to the question could be written as a number, it is quantitative. If the answer is a sentence a person says, it is qualitative.

EGYPTIAN EXAMPLE

Take the homework page you open every week. Suppose the numbers say: 60 students opened it (PV), 18 of them closed it without answering a single question (bounce rate of 30 percent), and 31 pressed send at the end (CVR of about 52 percent).

Those three numbers are worth a lot, and they still do not tell you one useful thing about what to fix. They say the losses are not at the start and not at the end, they are somewhere in the middle. The only way to learn what happens in the middle is to ask two of the eighteen: "you opened it and closed it, what happened?" One of them says the first round looked long on a phone. That sentence is the qualitative evaluation, and it is worth more than the three numbers, but you would never have known to ask that student without them.

3. Heuristic Evaluation

Heuristic evaluation is a method in which experts identify problems in a website's UI (user interface) based on rules of thumb (heuristics). It is a form of qualitative evaluation, used along with usability testing and other methods to investigate the causes of usability problems.

Two things in that definition are what the exam asks for. Who does it: experts, not ordinary users. What they check against: rules of thumb, established principles of good interface design, rather than their personal taste.

Major evaluation perspectives

PerspectiveWhat it asksAn example of a failure
Consistency Whether the design and operations are unified across the site. The button that saves is green on one page and grey on the next, and sits on the right here and on the left there.
Error prevention Whether the design makes it hard for users to make mistakes. A date field that silently accepts 31 February, or a Delete button directly beside Save.
Visibility of system status Whether system status is clearly indicated to users. You press send and nothing on the screen changes, so you press it again. No processing indicator and no input error message.

Heuristic evaluation معناها إن خبير (مش مستخدم عادي) يراجع الواجهة على قواعد معروفة (rules of thumb)، مش على ذوقه. وهي نوع من الـ qualitative لأن ناتجها كلام ومشاكل، مش أرقام.

GOING DEEPER

Heuristic evaluation is the one qualitative method that needs no users at all, which is exactly why teams reach for it first: it is fast and it is cheap. An expert walks the interface against the list and writes down every place a rule is broken.

Its weakness is the other side of the same coin. An expert can tell you that the status of the system is invisible; an expert cannot tell you that your users do not understand the word you chose for the button, because the expert already knows what it means. That is why the book places it alongside usability testing and not instead of it.

PAUSE & THINK

Which of the four rely on numbers, and which on people's words?

Numbers: web analytics, and A/B testing. Both are quantitative.

People's words and behaviour: usability testing, and interviews. Both are qualitative, and heuristic evaluation joins them as a third, with an expert in place of a user.

The one that most often gets sorted into the wrong column is A/B testing. It feels like an experiment with people, so it sounds qualitative. It is not: it decides between two versions by comparing the numbers each one produces, so it belongs with the quantitative methods.

Worked Example

FROM THE TEXTBOOK

(1) Mark each statement (A to D) with ○ if true or × if false.

A Qualitative evaluation is a method that uses numerical data to evaluate quantitatively.
B PV (Page View) is an indicator showing the number of times a page has been viewed.
C The bounce rate is an indicator showing the proportion of visits that achieved a target action.
D Heuristic evaluation is a method in which experts identify UI problems based on rules of thumb.

A: × false. This is the definition of the other method. Qualitative evaluation observes users' voices and behaviour to evaluate qualitatively; using numerical data is quantitative evaluation. The book swaps the two names and leaves everything else correct, which is the commonest trick in this lesson.

B: ○ true. Correct definition of PV.

C: × false. The proportion of visits that achieved a target action is the conversion rate. The bounce rate is the proportion of visits that ended after viewing only the first page. Two proportions, two different things being counted.

D: ○ true. Correct definition of heuristic evaluation: experts, and rules of thumb.

Exam-Style Question

FROM THE TEXTBOOK · 6 MARKS

Explain why a website team should use both qualitative and quantitative evaluation rather than only one. [6]

Refer to: what numbers show, and what users' voices show.

The model answer, one mark per sentence.

  1. Quantitative evaluation uses numerical data, such as PV, bounce rate and conversion rate, to show objectively how much a site is being used and on which pages visits are being lost.
  2. Because it is collected automatically from every visitor, it covers the whole site at once and shows the overall trend, which no other method can do.
  3. However, a number has no reason attached to it, so quantitative data can show that visitors leave a page but never why they leave it.
  4. Qualitative evaluation, through usability testing, interviews and heuristic evaluation, explores the reasons behind users' behaviour and gives the causes in the users' own words.
  5. But it is carried out with only a few users at a time, so on its own it cannot show whether the problem it found affects many visitors or just those few.
  6. Combining both is therefore more accurate: the numbers show where the problem is and how big it is, the voices explain why it happens, and after a change the numbers can be checked again to confirm whether the fix worked.

Where students lose marks here. Two definitions and nothing else is worth two marks out of six. The question is not "what are the two methods", it is why use both, so four of the six marks are in the limits: a number cannot say why, and a few users cannot say how many. Sentence 6 is the one that ties them together, and the "Refer to" line is the book telling you it expects both halves.

Think as an Engineer: Investigate & Decide

FROM THE TEXTBOOK

A small online bookstore in your city wants to know whether its website really works for customers. You plan how to evaluate it.

1 · Collect data. Ask five classmates who have used an online bookstore to visit any online shop and, for each, record one number you could measure (for example, how many pages they viewed) and one thing they said was confusing. Put the results in a small table.

2 · Analyse the signals. Suppose the bookstore's PV is high but its conversion rate is very low. What might that mean, and which method would tell you why? Then consider one customer the data may hide, and note one difficulty they might face that a raw PV count would never show.

3 · Decide. Recommend which two evaluation methods the bookstore should use first and give two reasons drawn from your table. Agree with a partner on the single most useful indicator to track.

Step 1, a worked table. Your own numbers will differ; what matters is that every row has a number on one side and a sentence on the other, because that is the whole lesson in one table.

PersonOne number I could measure One thing they said was confusing
AViewed 7 pages before finding a book "The categories and the search box are far apart."
BLeft after 1 page"The home page is all offers, I could not see any books."
CTook about 4 minutes to reach the basket "I could not tell whether the book was added."
DViewed 3 pages, bought nothing "Delivery cost only appeared at the last step."
EReached the last step, did not finish "It asked me to register before paying."

Step 2, what high PV with low CVR means. Plenty of people reach the site, so the problem is not attracting visitors; the problem is between arriving and buying. The number locates the gap and stops there, because a conversion rate cannot name a cause. The method that tells you why is qualitative: a usability test on the path from the product page to the finished order, or interviews with people who abandoned a basket. Person C and person E in the table above are already pointing at the two likeliest causes.

One customer the data may hide. Someone shopping on a small phone screen with a slow connection. A raw PV count treats their visit as identical to everyone else's, and may even count their repeated reloads as extra views, so the number goes up while their experience is getting worse. A customer using a screen reader is hidden the same way, and so is a first-time visitor who does not know the shop's wording. None of this appears in the figures, and all of it appears within two minutes of watching one such person try to buy a book.

Step 3, a defensible recommendation. Start with web analytics and a short usability test, in that order. First reason: analytics is already collecting data from every visitor at no cost, and it is the only method that can show which step of the purchase loses the most people, which is where the limited time should be spent. Second reason: three of the five people in the table described a problem that no number would ever reveal (the hidden delivery cost, the unclear basket, the forced registration), so a method that produces sentences is needed as well as one that produces figures.

The single most useful indicator: the conversion rate. PV says how many people came and bounce rate says how many left immediately, but a bookshop exists to sell books, so the one number that reflects whether the site is doing its job is the share of visits that end in a purchase. Any answer is acceptable here if the reason is tied to the site's purpose, which is what the question is really testing.

In a New Context

FROM THE TEXTBOOK

A school's new website gets many visits, but most people leave after the first page. Name one quantitative indicator that reveals this problem, one qualitative method to find its cause, and one likely fix.

The quantitative indicator: the bounce rate. It is defined as the proportion of visits that ended after viewing only the first page, which is exactly the behaviour described. The high visit count is the PV, and notice that the PV alone looks like good news; it is the bounce rate beside it that turns the same traffic into a problem.

The qualitative method: a usability test. Sit five parents in front of the home page and ask each one to find something real, for example next week's timetable or the exam dates, then watch without helping. Interviews are an equally good answer: ask people who visited what they came for and whether they found it.

One likely fix: put what visitors actually come for on the first page, with a clear link to it, instead of a welcome message and a photograph. If the test shows that parents arrive looking for the timetable and the home page opens with a greeting, the fix is to give the timetable a visible place at the top rather than to redesign the whole site.

What makes the answer complete: three parts are asked for and three must be named. A common half answer gives the bounce rate and then jumps straight to the fix, skipping the method. The marks are for the chain: the number shows there is a problem, the method finds the cause, and only then does a fix make sense.

Lesson Question, Answered

How can a website be evaluated, and why is it useful to combine numbers with users' voices?

Evaluation falls into two kinds. Quantitative evaluation uses numerical data, through web analytics indicators such as PV (how many views), bounce rate (how many leave after the first page) and CVR (how many reach a target action), to show objectively how much a site is used; A/B testing belongs here too, because it compares two versions by their numbers. Qualitative evaluation explores the reasons behind users' behaviour, through usability testing, interviews, and heuristic evaluation, in which experts check the interface against rules of thumb such as consistency, error prevention and visibility of system status.

Numbers reveal what is happening but not why; users' voices and expert checks reveal the causes but only for the few people who were asked. Combining the two, the "how much" and the "why", gives the most accurate evaluation and the clearest path to improvement: the numbers point to the page that is losing people, the voices explain what goes wrong there, and after the change the numbers say whether it worked.

Reflect, Challenge, and the Exercise

REFLECT

If you could see only one metric for a site you use, would you choose PV, bounce rate, or CVR, and why?

There is no single right answer; the mark is in the reason, and the reason has to come from what the site is for.

For a shop or a booking service, choose the CVR: the site exists so that people buy or reserve, so the share of visits that reach that action is the closest thing to a verdict on whether it works. For a news site or a school page, whose purpose is to be read, PV is defensible. For a site you suspect is broken rather than unvisited, bounce rate is the one that would reveal it fastest.

Whichever you pick, say what the metric would not tell you. That sentence is what separates a full answer from a preference.

CHALLENGE

A page has a high bounce rate. Design a short qualitative test that would reveal the cause, and say what you would change first.

The test. Five people, five minutes each. Give each one a real task that the page is supposed to serve, for example "find the price of delivery to your area", then ask them to say out loud what they are looking at and what they expect to happen. Do not help, do not explain the page, and write down the exact moment each person hesitates and the words they use. End with one question: "what did you expect to see here?"

Five is enough, which surprises people. You are not measuring anything, so you do not need a large sample; you are looking for the obstacle, and the same obstacle usually stops the second and third person as well.

What I would change first. Whatever stopped the most people at the earliest point. An obstacle in the first few seconds affects every visitor who arrives, while one at the last step affects only those who got that far, so fixing the earliest blocker moves the bounce rate most. Then measure again, because that is how you find out whether the explanation was right.
